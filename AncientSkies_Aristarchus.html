MISSION ATLAS · ANCIENT SKIES 1

Aristarchus’ Wager

Alexandria, ~250 BC. Aristarchus claims worlds can be measured without leaving Earth. Nausika, the merchant captain who funds the Library’s instruments, demands proof. You have a bead, a box with a hole, and one proportion.

D / d  =  s / L

One relationship. Real size over real distance equals bead size over bead distance.
Used four times: a lighthouse, the Moon, a distance, the Sun.

Mission brief — assumptions, units, scoring (tap to open)

Declared simplifications (Article 3): light travels in straight lines; the disks are treated as perfect circles; chord ≈ arc for such narrow angles (error ~0.001%); the Moon’s distance is tonight’s, not an average — it varies ±5.5%; distances you are given carry a declared origin; the bead position your hand finds carries the method’s real wobble (a few %) — that spread is the ancient method itself, and your medal never depends on it: you are graded on computing correctly from your own measurement.

Angular size (for lyceum): the ratio s/L is what astronomers call angular size — we simply never need the word to play.

Historical liberties: Nausika is fictional. Archimedes really did measure the rising Sun with a cylinder on a rod (Sand Reckoner) — we replace his direct viewing with a safe projection. Never look at the Sun.

Scoring (Article 4): 40% numerical prediction · 25% concept gate · 25% explanation · 10% confidence calibration. Medals: Gold <5% · Silver · Bronze. Language: English first; translations are separate passes.

Sources: Aristarchus, On Sizes and Distances; Archimedes, Sand Reckoner (preserves Aristarchus’ Moon at 1/720 of the circle).
